7½ Men · proposal for 2027

One draft,
one drum

Three changes that fit together: merge the two drafts into one, price kept rookies off the round they were actually taken in, and let the Chase bracket cost you lottery balls. Read it once, argue about it, then vote.

The change01

From 2027 we hold one draft. Rookies and veterans on the same board, one order, one lottery. Everything below follows from that.

2026 — what we did
  • Rookie draft: 2 rounds, 16 picks, its own lottery
  • Veteran draft: 13 rounds, its own lottery
  • Two drums, two sets of guardrails, no sweep rule
  • A rookie-draft pick has no veteran round, so R5 stands in as his keeper price
2027 — proposed
  • One draft, 16 rounds (14 + the 2 the rookie draft used)
  • One lottery, one drum
  • No sweep rule needed — there is nothing to sweep
  • A drafted rookie keeps at the round you actually took him in

Sixteen rounds keeps the total picks identical to this year: 14 + 2. With five keepers eating their rounds, a full team drafts eleven new players, which covers fifteen active spots and two taxi slots without anyone running out of roster.

What a kept rookie costs02

This is the part that needs saying out loud, because the answer is different for the players we already own than for everyone drafted from here.

The rule, in one line each

The 2026 rookie class — the sixteen taken in this year's rookie draft — enters the keeper system at round 5 if you keep him as a regular keeper.

Everyone drafted from 2027 on keeps at the round he was actually drafted in, rookie or not. R5 does not apply to them.

R5 was never meant to be permanent. It exists only because a rookie-draft pick had no round to price against — there was no seat on the veteran board with his name on it. Merge the drafts and that problem disappears: every player has a real round, so we use it. R5 becomes a legacy rule covering one class of sixteen players, and then it retires itself.

What this changes about how you draft

  • A late-round rookie becomes much more valuable to hold. Take a rookie in the 12th and he keeps at R12, not R5. That is seven rounds of profit a year that did not exist before.
  • An elite rookie becomes more expensive to hold. Take one in the 2nd and he keeps at R2. Under R5 he would have been cheap forever. This is the right way round: the board should price him, not a flat number we invented.
  • Rookie keeper slots have to change too, or consolidation breaks them. They cost your last round today, which is safe only because the most you can spend on a rookie is a 2nd-round pick off a separate 16-pick board. Put rookies on the main board and you could take one at 1.01 and keep him at R14 for his entire career — thirteen rounds of profit a year, forever, with no wall. See below.

And the rookie keeper slot itself

The fix

A rookie keeper costs the round you drafted him in, the same as everyone else. The perk becomes the missing three-year clock — you hold him his whole career — rather than a free price.

This keeps the thing rookie keepers are for and removes the thing that would break them. Find a rookie in the 14th who turns into a first-rounder and you bank thirteen rounds a year with no wall — that is the whole point, and it is untouched. Buy one at 1.01 and you pay full freight to hold him, which is the part that has to change the moment rookies share a board with everyone else.

The lottery, rebuilt03

One draft means one drum. That deletes two rules on its own — the no-sweep rule has nothing to sweep, and "no back-to-back, per drum" becomes simply no back-to-back.

The interesting part is what replaces them. Today the veteran drum runs on final standing so a Chase win quietly costs you veteran balls. With one drum we make that explicit and sharper: winning in the Chase bracket costs you lottery balls.

Proposed

The drum is seeded on regular-season record, worst first, exactly as now. Then the Chase bracket re-sorts the four ball weights held by the teams that missed the playoffs — the Chase winner takes the smallest of the four, the team that loses out takes the largest.

That is the whole idea in one number: winning the Chase costs you 9.4 percentage points of first choice and pays you up to $120. Neither answer is obviously right, which is exactly what makes it a decision rather than a formality. Weeks 15 to 17 stop being a lap of honour for the teams that missed out.

The four playoff teams are unaffected — 10 / 7 / 3 / 2 by finish, champion floored at the smallest weight, same as now.

The variant, if you want every game to count

The above is decided by where you finish. If we would rather every individual game carry weight, the alternative is each Chase win costs 3 balls — one seed's worth — with a floor so no Chase team ever drops below the best-placed playoff loser. Same incentive, finer grain, one more number to remember on the night.
